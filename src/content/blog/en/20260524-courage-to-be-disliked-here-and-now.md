## The night I overthought "three years from now"

If you've been building a career as an engineer, you'll recognize this kind of night.

*"Where do I want to be in three years?"*
*"Am I actually compounding my skills?"*
*"My peers seem to be further along."*

Just the other night I scribbled *"5-year career plan"* in a notebook, and by the time I was done, the writing had somehow made the anxiety worse, not better.

Imagine the future too hard, and the present gets thin.
Want recognition too much, and today's work turns into a duty.

The last night of *The Courage to Be Disliked* (Night V) hands you a prescription aimed exactly at this state.
[Part 1](/HomePage/en/blog/20260522-courage-to-be-disliked-teleology/) covered teleology and [Part 2](/HomePage/en/blog/20260523-courage-to-be-disliked-separation-of-tasks/) the separation of tasks. This is the closing essay of the three-part series.

## Live "now, here" seriously

The single line in this book that I've returned to the most:

> **Life is not a line. It is a series of points.**
> **What matters is to live this moment seriously, as if dancing.**

Importantly, this is **not** "don't think about the future."
What the book is pushing back on is the **trade where you sacrifice the present for the future** — the posture of *"once X happens, I'll start for real."*

- Once the project settles down, I'll study
- Once I change jobs, I'll really go for it
- Once I'm promoted, I'll start writing publicly

Each of these, the book quietly points out, is a **different shape of the same excuse not to live now**.

All we actually have is one point — now — lived as if we were dancing.
The trail those points leave behind is what, in retrospect, we call a "life."

## The three practices — self-acceptance, trust, contribution

For grounding "now, here," the book offers three concrete postures. This is the climax of the work.

### 1. Self-acceptance

This is not "love yourself just as you are."
The book is sharper than that:

> **Accept what you cannot change. Have the courage to change what you can.**

For me:

- Can't change: my age (27), the career I've already built, decisions I got wrong in the past
- Can change: how I spend the next 30 minutes, what I ask in tomorrow's 1-on-1, whether I write a blog post today

That one sort turns a vague *"is my current life okay?"* into a **list you can actually act on.**

### 2. Trust in others

The book distinguishes **trust** from **credit**.
*Credit* is conditional (track record, money returned, etc.).
*Trust* is believing in someone without conditions.

In a work context:

- The team member delivered progress, so I'll believe them → credit
- I assume the team member's intent and capability regardless of whether they've just delivered → trust

Trust comes with the risk of being betrayed. But unless you stand on the trust side, the book argues, you cannot build a **horizontal relationship**.
This is the same view I described in [the Reinventing Organizations note](/HomePage/en/blog/20260518-teal-organization/) — the move from pyramid to self-driven.

### 3. Contribution

And the last key — **contribution to others.**

> **The feeling that "I am being useful to someone" is what makes a person happy.**

The key distinction: this is **not** the same as approval.
Approval requires **other people's evaluation**. Contribution is complete **in your own subjective sense** of it.

For me, writing a blog post every day is held up almost entirely by this subjective sense of contribution.

- Maybe someone will read it
- Maybe future-me will use it to think more clearly
- Maybe one person dealing with the same thing will catch a sentence that helps

No guarantees on any of those. But in the moment of writing, if I can think *"I am making something that might reach someone right now,"* contribution has already occurred — per the book.

## Re-reading "writing a blog every day" through this book

Lining those three up, the habit I've been quietly running looks different.

| What I do | Adler's reading of it |
| --- | --- |
| Write a blog post every day | Living **now, here** as a point, seriously |
| Publish without waiting for perfection | **Self-acceptance**: accept the writing skill I can't change today, choose what I can (write or don't) |
| Publish even when I don't know who reads | **Trust in others**: assume the reader, unconditionally |
| Keep going even when there's no reaction | **Contribution**: not approval — my own subjective sense of usefulness |

Strange thing: after the book, the habit itself has started to function as **a prescription**, not a hobby.

The detour of "turning experience into formal knowledge" I described in [When I realized my biggest gap wasn't technical](/HomePage/en/blog/20260519-real-gap-isnt-technical/) is, in this language, just stacking points where I can — "now, here."

## The closing prescription for that career-plan night

Back to the "three years from now" panic at the top.

*The Courage to Be Disliked* would say something like this:

> **Sketch where you want to be in three years if you like.**
> **Just don't use that sketch as a reason to sacrifice now.**

I've been telling myself, lately:

- A three-year map takes about five minutes to draw
- Only the present-me can decide how the next 30 minutes are spent
- All "three-year selves" are built from those 30-minute increments

Look at the future while spending the next 30 minutes **like a dance**, seriously.
That is the only investment in the future we can actually make.

## Summary

- The core of *The Courage to Be Disliked* is **living "now, here" as a point**, seriously. Stop narrating life as a line
- What the book pushes back on is the disguised excuse: *"once X, I'll start for real"*
- The three practices: **self-acceptance / trust in others / contribution to others**. Contribution is subjective — unlike approval
- Writing a blog daily, running quiet 1-on-1s, small contributions — these are all the act of stacking "now, here" points
- A future map is quick to draw. **Spend the long time on how today's 30 minutes are used.**

For three articles I've walked through my own bible, *The Courage to Be Disliked*, alongside how I actually work.
The book rewards re-reading — every pass produces a new line. If I had to leave one sentence here, it would be this:

> **It's okay to be disliked. Quietly keep doing what makes you feel you are contributing — now, here.**

— Tomorrow I'll be back, putting down another point.
