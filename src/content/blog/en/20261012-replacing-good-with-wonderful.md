I think of praise as a word sent from above to below. One moment on the job made me stop saying "that's a good idea." This post walks through that experience and the thinking behind the shift.

## "That's a very good idea"

While reviewing a senior teammate's work, I suggested, "If we restructure this table, it would be easier for readers to see what's going on and where the information is. What do you think?" The reply was: "That's a very good idea."

It's a small nuance, nothing more. But to me, "good" carried a faint echo of hierarchy, as if someone with the standing to judge had judged. The structure of evaluator and evaluated showed through, just slightly, underneath the words.

## Why I say "wonderful" instead of "good"

In the flat team I'm aiming for, that sentence would change. Not "that's a very good idea," but "that's a very wonderful idea."

In a top-down structure, hierarchy is the premise, so praise tends toward judgments like good or bad. "Wonderful" is different: it's not a judgment, it's a feeling that simply rose up in me. Judgment needs someone holding the standard and someone being measured against it. A feeling needs neither. That's why, when someone's idea or opinion moves me, I say "that's wonderful" rather than "that's good."

## The flat team I'm building toward

I see teams not as top-down hierarchies but as cross-functional structures laid side by side. Not one top manager giving orders to members who follow, but a manager who manages, reviews, and supports, standing alongside members who execute tasks and share information. It's close to the idea I found in [my reading notes on Teal organizations](/HomePage/en/blog/20260518-teal-organization/): teams that run themselves once authority is delegated.

Right now, I lead two teams of six people at work, while also managing and contributing within one of those teams myself. Evaluating and reviewing people's work comes up constantly, which makes word choice a real, practical problem. Working through [a four-stage model for psychological safety](/HomePage/en/blog/20260521-designing-psychological-safety/) — understand, design, implement, reproduce — taught me that the feeling of "being evaluated" can itself erode safety.

Underneath this is a wish: a society where people can hold onto dreams and say them out loud. That's also why I built [YumeHashi](/HomePage/en/blog/20260408-yumehashi-story/), my personal project — to grow places where people can speak honestly about what they want. Words that carry hierarchy chip away at that honesty, little by little.

## "Thank you" works the same way

There's one more phrase I use deliberately: "thank you." Like "wonderful," it's a feeling rising up from inside me, not a judgment, so it carries no hierarchy either.

When someone finishes a task I asked for and requests a review, I say, "thanks for finishing that and asking for a review." If they did it without waiting to be told, even more so. When I'm the one assigning a task, I always open with, "thanks for always taking on what I ask." As I wrote in [an earlier post about words as blades](/HomePage/en/blog/20260412-power-of-words/), words keep living inside the person who received them long after they're spoken. That's exactly why I pay attention to which ones I choose.

## Why gratitude works: reciprocity and framing

There's psychological backing for this. The **reciprocity principle** holds that people who receive goodwill want to return it. Receiving goodwill triggers a "I shouldn't betray this" instinct, which makes a person more open to feedback and more willing to return the favor.

The other is the **framing effect**: the same message lands very differently depending on how it's presented. "Do this" breeds resistance. "Thanks for doing this" invites the same request to be received well.

## Conclusion: words shape relationships

Avoiding "good," a word with a trace of hierarchy in it, and choosing "wonderful" or "thank you" instead — words with no hierarchy at all — is a small practice of mine. But I believe it's a real step toward a much larger goal: building an organization.

Culture doesn't change through one person's effort alone. That's exactly why I hope this kind of word choice gradually takes root among the people on my teams too. The concrete steps are still a work in progress, but choosing words carefully remains, for me, part of the foundation for growing a psychologically safe team.
