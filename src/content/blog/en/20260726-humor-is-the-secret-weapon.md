I've long felt that bringing humor into work matters. Not humor as a talent for making people laugh or hyping up a room — something quieter and more human: taking the edge off a tense moment, closing a small gap of distance with someone, leaving a little slack in your own mind.

*Humor, Seriously*, by Jennifer Aaker and Naomi Bagdonas, put language to that feeling. What stuck with me most was the idea that **seriousness and levity aren't opposites**. I'd unconsciously linked being conscientious at work with having no room to breathe, so this reframing felt like relief.

## Don't let levity get crowded out by seriousness

I'd assumed, without quite deciding to, that seriousness should lead at work — being sincere, being careful, not making mistakes — and that levity gets pushed to the margins as a result.

But the book's point is that deepening your seriousness and losing your humanity are, in fact, separate tracks. Chase seriousness alone and work turns into a contest of being right, quietly draining slack from everyone involved, yourself included. The authors cite research showing adults' daily laughter drops sharply starting around age 23. What struck me wasn't just the number — it's that **growing up seems to make it harder to give yourself permission to be lighthearted at all.**

## Humor isn't about getting laughs — it's about being appropriate

I used to flinch a little at the word "humor." It conjured someone quick with a line, someone who can work a room — clearly not me, I assumed.

The book reframes this: **exercising levity is not the same as getting a laugh.** What matters is being appropriate to the moment, and letting your humanity show without undercutting your competence or status. Humor turns out to be less an entertainment skill and more a facet of how someone shows up. That reframe alone dissolved most of my hesitation. The book sketches four humor styles — Stand-Up, Sweetheart, Magnet, Sniper — some people carry a room, others land one quiet line. "Becoming a funny person" felt like the wrong goal; "finding the style that's actually mine" felt like the right one.

## Humor as an entry point to psychological safety

What pulled me in most was the link between humor and psychological safety. The book notes that laughing together releases dopamine and oxytocin, making moments more memorable and people feel closer. Humor doesn't just lighten the room — **it signals to the other person that it's safe to relax here.**

As I wrote in [Knowing psychological safety isn't enough](/HomePage/en/blog/20260521-designing-psychological-safety/), the core of psychological safety is being able to say the hard thing. A rigid room makes even correct statements hard to voice; a slightly looser one makes half-formed, unconfident ideas easier to offer. The book is careful to note humor isn't a cure-all, though — stripped of context or relationship, it just reads as friction or discomfort.

People who can admit their own mistakes carry a kind of slack with them. People who insist on always being right, less so. When a leader in particular can own a mistake and put themselves in relative terms, the room's whole temperature shifts — an idea that echoes what I wrote in [Failure Science](/HomePage/en/blog/20260419-failure-science/) about building a culture that turns failure into learning.

## What today's leaders need isn't polish — it's a human, relatable presence

The part that made me think hardest was about leadership itself. The book argues that what's demanded of leaders today isn't a display of competence — it's being genuine and relatable. Being capable and having good judgment still matter, but they're not enough on their own to earn people's loyalty. Right now, I think, people trust someone they feel safe working alongside — someone who actually sees them — more than someone who's simply skilled.

The humor here isn't about being funny enough to earn praise. It's adding a little lightness to a room built entirely of tension. Adding a little humanity to a conversation strung tight with correctness. Small adjustments like these, stacked up, are what build trust and a sense of safety. Not leaving a relationship on a tense note connects, I think, to what I wrote about [a manager's job being asking, not answering](/HomePage/en/blog/20260509-art-of-asking-questions/) — the questions a leader asks shape the room as much as the answers they give.

## Closing thoughts

*Humor, Seriously* changed how I see humor. I used to file it under "something funny people have." Now I hold it as **a way of keeping your humanity intact inside seriousness.**

I still want to take sincerity seriously at work. But sincerity shouldn't have to mean no slack, no approachability. Humor, in this book's sense, isn't a performance skill — it's adding a slightly different ingredient to your life and work. I'd like to be someone who holds both: seriousness, and a little levity inside it.
