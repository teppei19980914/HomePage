I first read *The Courage to Be Disliked* in my second year of high school. About ten years later, I reread it, this time while using my reading log in [Michinari](/HomePage/en/blog/20260831-michinari-story/). What surprised me most was that **it was the same book, but the parts that stayed with me were completely different.**

## In high school, I took away "acceptance"

Back then, I was walking the path others expected of me, moving toward grades and praise. That's where I encountered the **separation of tasks**. I focused on what I could change, like which university to apply to, and stopped looking at what I couldn't change, like how my parents would judge that choice. As a result, I could make peace with any outcome, because I had actively chosen the path myself.

My reading back then was an answer to one question: **how do I take responsibility for my own choices?**

## Now, I was pulled back toward "contribution"

What resonated most this time was the idea that self-acceptance, trust in others, and contribution to others lead to community feeling, and with it, a happiness rooted in a sense of belonging. Contribution to others struck me the hardest.

I've long compared myself not with other people, but with my past self. Today against last year, next year against today. Reading the book, I started to feel that this accumulation is what made me who I am now.

I also nodded hard at the passage saying that even the super-rich keep working because, through contributing to others, they keep confirming their sense of belonging. I'd seen the same idea in other books too. Meeting the same conclusion in more than one place made me feel it was not a mistaken idea.

## The separation of tasks wasn't enough

I [wrote earlier about the separation of tasks](/HomePage/en/blog/20260523-courage-to-be-disliked-separation-of-tasks/) and how it stopped me from trying to move other people. That was necessary for getting my freedom back.

But this reread made me realize that **"giving up on moving other people" and "giving up on engaging with other people" are different things.** The first is a way to stop wearing yourself out. If you slide into the second, relationships thin out. The community feeling the book describes is a state where you see the people in front of you as companions and feel that you have a place among them. And a sense of belonging isn't something someone hands you. It's something you gain through the sense of contribution you feel when you engage with a community and are useful to it.

If the separation of tasks is about taking back the steering wheel, then contribution to others is about deciding where to go with it, and with whom.

## As a leader, turning contribution into a system

I lead two teams. Trust in others matters, but what I felt most strongly this time is that contribution to others matters most. I want to value the people on the front lines, and build their voices into the systems we work with.

When I wrote about [once seeing myself as an interchangeable tool](/HomePage/en/blog/20260912-heidegger-finitude-team-building/), I was pointing in the same direction. Don't treat people as tools for a purpose. And contribution, I now think, isn't about meeting someone's expectations. It's about carrying the voices from the front line into the structures that shape our work.

## The same book, becoming a different book

The high school version of me took back the steering wheel of my life through the separation of tasks. The version of me today is looking for a place among others through contribution. Both are part of this book.

The book didn't change. I did. I think that's exactly why it's worth rereading the same book: it reflects the change back to you.

## Closing

*The Courage to Be Disliked* turned out not to be a book you read once and finish. "Focus on your own tasks" now feels like it's asking me how I use that focus for someone else. The next time I read it, I'm sure a different line will catch on me.
