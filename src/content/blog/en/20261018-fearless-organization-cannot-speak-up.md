I'm rereading one of the books I cited in [my internal seminar post](/HomePage/en/blog/20261015-psychological-safety-internal-seminar-recap/): Amy Edmondson's *The Fearless Organization*. This time I want to dig into two points that stuck with me — the kinds of learning that psychological safety actually helps, and the Volkswagen scandal.

## It's not about fewer mistakes — it's about whether people can report them

The book's starting point, which I also touched on in [an earlier post](/HomePage/en/blog/20260521-designing-psychological-safety/), is a paradox: higher-performing teams report more mistakes, not fewer. Lower-performing teams can't openly report mistakes, so fewer surface — which only makes them look like they have fewer problems. Edmondson names this gap "psychological safety."

What was new to me this time was the distinction between two kinds of learning. **Learn-what** is self-directed: catching up on information on your own. **Learn-how** is team-based: sharing knowledge, proposing ideas, brainstorming together. Psychological safety showed no correlation with learn-what. But it strongly enabled learn-how, by making it easier to overcome the interpersonal risk involved in speaking up in front of others. In other words, psychological safety doesn't help you study alone — it helps a team think together.

## The Volkswagen scandal: silence is not the same as "all is well"

The book uses Volkswagen's emissions scandal as a case where this structure collapsed entirely.

VW's leadership believed that no news meant things were going well, and that hitting targets required exercising power over people. Together, these two assumptions produced an effectively impossible mandate: build a car that meets strict emissions standards in six weeks, or everyone gets fired. Engineers responded by falsifying test results and shipping cars that didn't actually meet the standard. When the fraud came out, the company's credibility collapsed.

What struck me most was Edmondson's reframing: in a low-safety organization, people below aren't "choosing not to speak" or "deciding they shouldn't." They **can't**. That distinction matters. People at the top rarely notice they're the ones silencing the people below them — the absence of dissent quietly convinces them their targets must have been reasonable after all.

And this kind of failure never explodes out of nowhere. The seeds take root for months, sometimes years, before they surface. A culture of silence is, by definition, a dangerous one.

## Discussion isn't something to win

The book also addresses how discussion itself should work: it's a space where people with different experience and authority open up ideas and possibilities together — not a contest to be won.

One line stayed with me more than any other: recognizing your own mistake is proof that you're learning, and that's worth more than being right.

## Bringing it back to my own work

In software projects with real uniqueness and real deadlines, clinging to the old way of doing things stops being an option. Moving a project forward requires trying new approaches, and trying new approaches always carries the risk of failure. Without psychological safety, people fear that risk, and innovation simply stops happening. That's why I want to keep welcoming feedback from teammates, good or bad, without exception.

Noticing your own mistake and owning it, regardless of role or hierarchy, is close in spirit to what I wrote in ["Trust Comes Before Technical Skill for Engineers"](/HomePage/en/blog/20260805-trust-before-technical-skill/) — sharing mistakes honestly instead of hiding them.

## Takeaways

- Psychological safety mainly fuels team-based learning (learn-how), not solo learning (learn-what)
- The VW scandal shows the gap between "won't speak" and "can't speak"
- People at the top rarely notice they're the ones silencing people below them
- A culture of silence takes months or years to build — and is dangerous by nature
- Owning your own mistake matters more than being right
