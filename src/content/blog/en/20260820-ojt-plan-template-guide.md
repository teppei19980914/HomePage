As part of the training I described in [OJT Isn't Teaching — It's Designing a Path to Autonomy](/HomePage/en/blog/20260817-ojt-design-for-autonomy/), I built a six-month OJT plan template as my training exercise. This post walks through how to actually use it.

## 1. Basic info and goal-setting

The template opens with basic details — trainee, trainer, target system. The most important part here is the **goal statement**. The template's format is: "having understood ___, within ___ months have the trainee be able to ___ alone, without ___'s support." Three things matter: write it in **first person** ("the trainee can..."), in a **completed state** ("can explain," "can produce"), and in a way that's **objectively judgeable** ("without support," "alone"). "Try hard" or "understand" gives no one a way to judge whether it was achieved. A concrete goal is the starting point for the entire plan.

## 2. Phase-by-phase plan

Once the goal is set, you work backward to design a staged learning cycle. The template runs through five phases — why it exists, what it can do, how it's built, connecting the dots into one document, and explaining it solo — each with a slot for "duration," "purpose," "learning cycle," and a checklist of the "state to reach" by the end. The phase order itself is meant to stay fixed as a core principle, while the duration split and the specifics of each learning cycle are meant to flex based on how complex the work is and how experienced the trainee already is.

## 3. Coaching and support methods

This section maps trainer actions to each phase. For phase 1, for example: narrow the scope of what to investigate before assigning it, have the trainee explain it in their own words at a weekly check-in, and when something's wrong, don't hand over the correction — ask a question that leads them to notice it themselves, down to sample prompts like "what happens in the X case?" Each method ends with a checklist the trainer can revisit periodically — did I leave "go look into it" as the whole instruction, am I supporting how they think rather than just handing over the right answer.

## 4. Progress tracking and final review

Progress tracking logs planned versus actual duration and status per phase, plus a running **check-in log** — date, phase, the trainee's state, the trainer's feedback, and the action items before the next check-in. Final review doesn't stop at a simple achieved / partially achieved / not achieved call — it scores multiple dimensions, from understanding the business context to handling live Q&A, and records both the **trainee's self-assessment** and the **trainer's overall evaluation**. Keeping a record of the whole process that you can look back on later ends up useful for whoever you train next, too.

## 5. Using and adapting the template

The usage flow is simple: (1) fill in basic info, (2) agree on the goal together with the trainee, (3) fill in duration and target state for each phase, (4) revisit the checklist during coaching, (5) update progress tracking weekly or biweekly, (6) run the final review at completion. Phase count, learning cycle specifics, coaching detail, and evaluation criteria are all meant to be adjusted to fit the work, the trainer's own experience, and the team's culture.

What matters isn't filling out every field to the letter. **As long as the phase order stays intact — understand the purpose, then the features, then the design, then integrate the knowledge, then explain it independently** — everything else can flex to match what's actually happening on the ground. The checklists are a tool for reflecting on the quality of your coaching, not a target to fully check off.

## Closing thoughts

Putting this into a template made me appreciate having a shape to work from. Coach without any structure, and you end up judging progress purely by what's visible from the trainee's side. With a chain of goal-setting, phase design, coaching methods, progress tracking, and evaluation in place, you can keep revisiting and adjusting the coaching itself as you go.

The template is a starting point, nothing more. Hold onto the core idea from [OJT Isn't Teaching — It's Designing a Path to Autonomy](/HomePage/en/blog/20260817-ojt-design-for-autonomy/) — designing the conditions for autonomy, not just teaching — and let this template grow into whatever shape fits your own team.
