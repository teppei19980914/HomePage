Continuing from [my last post](/HomePage/en/blog/20261018-fearless-organization-cannot-speak-up/), these are more reading notes on Amy Edmondson's *The Fearless Organization*. This time I want to go one level deeper than the "three basic leader behaviors" I briefly mentioned in [my internal seminar post](/HomePage/en/blog/20261015-psychological-safety-internal-seminar-recap/).

## A leader's duties break down into three categories, nine items

The book organizes the toolkit for leaders building psychological safety into three categories.

| Category | Leader's duties | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Set the stage | ① Frame the work (treat failure, uncertainty, and interdependence as given; make clear why candor is needed) ② Highlight what's at stake (be clear on what's at risk, and why and for whom it matters) | Shared expectations and meaning |
| Invite participation | ① Show situational humility (admit you're not perfect) ② Ask inquiring questions (model focused listening, ask good questions) ③ Set up structures and processes (forums for input, discussion guidelines) | Confidence that speaking up is welcome |
| Respond productively | ① Express appreciation (listen, accept, thank) ② Destigmatize failure (offer support, look forward, discuss) ③ Sanction clear violations | Orientation toward continuous learning |

It's easy to miss that "respond productively" includes sanctioning clear violations. Psychological safety isn't about tolerating everything.

## Situational humility and inclusive leadership

The item I found most actionable was "situational humility": believing learning never ends, and never assuming you know everything. A leader without it lets "knowing everything" show through in how they act — and people stop wanting to bring their thoughts to someone like that.

The book defines the "inclusive leadership" needed to practice this through three traits: being approachable and easy to talk to; recognizing yourself as an imperfect, fallible person; and asking for others' opinions in a way that makes it easy for them to speak. To draw speech out of others, a leader has to set aside their own fear that asking a question might make them look ignorant or incompetent, and ask it anyway.

Questions themselves have conditions too. The book's three traits of a "powerful question": you genuinely don't know the answer; it can't be answered with a simple yes or no; and it lets the other person focus and share their own thinking. This overlaps with what I wrote in ["From Funeral to Adventure: A Manager's Real Art of Asking"](/HomePage/en/blog/20260509-art-of-asking-questions/), but the book's insistence that the question must come from genuinely not knowing the answer struck me as the sharper point.

## Failure is "bound to happen" — the Swiss cheese model and the paradox of defenses

Fear of failure is the biggest signal that an organization's psychological safety is low. Unless a leader actively and explicitly makes it safe to fail, people will inevitably avoid failure.

This connects to the "Swiss cheese model": whatever system you put in place, holes always appear somewhere. An accident only happens when the holes in multiple layers of defense happen to line up. That's exactly why it matters that people understand failure as something bound to happen, not a personal fault.

At the same time, adding too many safeguards creates its own danger: the "paradox of defenses." Overly detailed procedures lead people to stop actually following them, which in turn creates the conditions for unexpected failures. Avoiding this paradox while still protecting safety comes down to balancing two kinds of control: **external control** (rules, standards, and procedures imposed from outside the individual) and **internal control** (knowledge and principles gained through training and experience, which naturally shape behavior from within).

## Bringing it back to my own work

In software development, I've noticed the same pattern when review checklists and gates keep growing: people start going through the motions instead of actually checking anything meaningful. The paradox of defenses puts a name on that exact feeling. Relying only on external controls like checklists isn't enough — building the internal control where people understand *why* a check matters, through experience, is ultimately the shorter path to fewer incidents.

Leaders in a VUCA world need to offer direction, not answers, which means they themselves must keep learning continuously. That's why I want to keep encouraging people to share even uncertain concerns, and to drop my own assumption that asking a question is something to be embarrassed about. This lines up closely with the "implement" stage I described in [a four-stage model for psychological safety](/HomePage/en/blog/20260521-designing-psychological-safety/).

## Takeaways

- A leader's duties fall into three categories and nine items: set the stage, invite participation, respond productively
- Psychological safety includes sanctioning violations — it isn't about tolerating everything
- Inclusive leadership and "powerful questions" are the concrete behaviors that invite participation
- Failure is bound to happen (Swiss cheese model) — but over-applying safeguards creates its own paradox of defenses
- Balancing external and internal control is what makes safety and flexibility coexist
