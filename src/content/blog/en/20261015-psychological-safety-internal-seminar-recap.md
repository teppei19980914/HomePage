As a lead overseeing two teams, I recently ran an internal seminar titled "Building Teams Through Psychological Safety," about 50 minutes long. It was my first chance to speak systematically, outside my own team, about the ideas I've been developing in [a four-stage model for psychological safety](/HomePage/en/blog/20260521-designing-psychological-safety/) — understand, design, implement, reproduce. While preparing the slides, I labeled every claim either **[Fact]**, something confirmed in research or books, or **[My take]**, my own interpretation or suggestion, so the audience could tell evidence apart from opinion. This post summarizes what I covered.

## Not the same thing as a friendly team

The most widely cited definition comes from Harvard Business School professor Amy Edmondson's 1999 paper: "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking." Three things matter here: it's a **team-level** state, not an individual trait; it's a **shared belief**, not just the leader's own impression; and it concerns **interpersonal risk** — asking questions, admitting mistakes, voicing disagreement.

Crucially, this is a different concept from cohesion, or simply "getting along." Too much cohesion can backfire into groupthink, making dissent harder to voice. In fact, Edmondson's 1996 study of hospital nursing teams found a paradox: teams with higher-rated teamwork had **more reported errors**, not fewer. Her interpretation: reported errors are a function of both actual errors and how easy it is to report them. It makes me ask — are teams with few incident reports really higher quality, or just less able to speak up? Counting incidents alone isn't enough.

## Aim for the "learning zone," not leniency

Psychological safety is often misread as "never saying anything harsh" or "lowering the bar." It's closer to the opposite. Edmondson's four-zone model plots psychological safety against the level of standards and accountability. Low on both is the "apathy zone." Safe but low standards is the "comfort zone." High standards without safety is the "anxiety zone," where mistakes stay hidden. High on both is the **"learning zone"** — the state worth aiming for. Psychological safety is the foundation for reaching a high standard, not a way to avoid one.

## Does it backfire at high levels? Taking the limits seriously

Presenting only the upside would undercut the talk's credibility. A 2023 study (Eldor, Hodor & Cappelli) reported that in routine, standardized work, psychological safety helps performance up to a moderate level, but too much of it can actually hurt performance — though a team's sense of collective accountability cushions that drop (the framing of this study has itself drawn criticism from practitioners, and the debate continues). Most research in this area is correlational, too, so it's not accurate to claim "more always means better results." My own conclusion: psychological safety needs to be paired with **accountability and standards**, and tuned to the nature of the work.

## A small step you can take tomorrow

In *The Fearless Organization*, Edmondson outlines three leader behaviors for building psychological safety: ① **framing the work** — explaining uncertainty and why speaking up matters; ② **asking for participation** — admitting your own blind spots and asking questions; ③ **responding productively** — thanking people for speaking up, treating failure as a learning opportunity, while still responding firmly to clear violations. That last point matters: it's not about tolerating everything.

Small shifts in everyday language help too. Swap "why did this happen?" for "let's figure out what happened, together." Swap "that's not possible" for "what would it take to make this work?" This connects to the same idea I explored in [why I stopped saying "good idea"](/HomePage/en/blog/20261012-replacing-good-with-wonderful/). If there's one thing to take away, it's this: when someone brings you bad news, say "thanks for telling me early" first.

## Conclusion

Psychological safety isn't about being friendly; it's a team-wide shared belief that interpersonal risk is safe to take. It drives growth and performance through learning behavior, but only when paired with accountability and matched to the nature of the work. Building it comes down to three behaviors — framing the work, asking for participation, responding productively — plus small, everyday word choices. As I wrote in [humor as the ultimate weapon](/HomePage/en/blog/20260726-humor-is-the-secret-weapon/), it's that foundation that makes room for challenge and new ideas to emerge in the first place.
